How do you read a reading-level chart by grade?

A reading-level chart by grade is a reference that relates a named reading assessment or text-leveling system to school grades. Its meaning depends on the system, the reference population and the assessment date. Use it to ask better questions about a report, rather than to assign a child a permanent reading label.

Which system is the report using?

Before looking for a chart, copy the exact assessment name from the report. A letter, a Lexile measure and a school’s own description are different kinds of information. A chart that mixes them without explaining its sources hides those differences. The Lexile publisher’s chart, linked below, is one example of a reference for a particular system.

  1. Identify the assessment and testing date.
  2. Check whether the chart describes readers or books.
  3. Ask whether the comparison is local, national or tied to a specific curriculum.

What about Fountas & Pinnell and Canadian grades?

For a Fountas & Pinnell result, ask the school for the interpretation attached to that assessment and the actual books used. For a Canadian report, start with the province, school board and classroom expectations. This guide does not supply an unsupported country-wide conversion table. Bring a recent piece of reading work to the conversation so the chart is connected to something the learner actually read.

How can a family use the chart constructively?

Ask what the learner can already do independently and what still requires support. A learner may recognize words accurately yet have trouble explaining the passage. Another may understand a read-aloud well but find decoding demanding. Those observations point to different practice choices. Keep the original report and teacher explanation together instead of replacing them with a number from an unrelated online quiz.

How can you find a level, and is there a highest reading age?

For a reported level, identify the assessment that produced it and ask the school or adult educator how to interpret the result. To choose practice material without a report, try a short passage and explain its meaning; that observation is not a standardized score. A reading-age label needs the name and limits of the test behind it. Do not read the highest label on a chart as a universal ceiling on someone’s learning.

Is there one universal reading-level chart?

No single chart on this site combines all systems. Use the publisher or school reference for the named assessment.

Should a chart determine every book a child reads?

Use it as context. Interests, subject knowledge and the support available also matter when choosing a book.
